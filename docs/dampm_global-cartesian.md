# DAMPM global cartesian model

The **DAMPM global** model (`sim_model: "dampm-global-cartesian"`, short `"global"`) is the depth-averaged material
point method of avalanchers. It treats the dense flow as a thin layer of snow with constant density $\rho$
that slides over the bed given by the DEM. Mass and momentum balances are integrated over the flow thickness,
as in Hutter et al. (1993). The flow is stored on the horizontal grid of the DEM, but its velocity is a
three-dimensional Cartesian vector kept tangent to the bed. No local bed-aligned coordinate system is needed:
the terrain enters the equations only through the bed normal and the bed curvature.

The equations are solved with a material point method (MPM, Sulsky et al. 1994): particles carry mass and
momentum, while all forces are evaluated on the fixed DEM grid.

The two other grid models share most of this description:

| Model                   | `sim_model`                                                   | Velocity                                          |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| DAMPM global            | `dampm-global-cartesian`, `global`                          | 3D Cartesian, tangent to the bed                  |
| DAMPM terrain-following | `dampm-terrain-following`, `terrain-following`, `terrain` | 2D, in the surface plane                          |
| DAMPM curvilinear       | `dampm-curvilinear`, `curvilinear`, `curvi`               | horizontal `(dx/dt, dy/dt)` with surface metric |

See [model conventions](model_conventions.md) for the velocity conventions of the other two.

## Bed geometry and flow variables

The DEM provides the elevation $z$ on a regular grid with cell size $\Delta$. A point on the bed has the
horizontal position $\mathbf{x} = (x, y)$ and the elevation $z(\mathbf{x})$. The gradient uses central
differences,

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} \approx \frac{z_{i+1,j} - z_{i-1,j}}{2\Delta}, \qquad
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} \approx \frac{z_{i,j+1} - z_{i,j-1}}{2\Delta}.
$$

Unit bed normal $\mathbf{n}$ and slope angle $\theta$:

$$
\mathbf{n} = \frac{(-\partial_x z,\ -\partial_y z,\ 1)}{\sqrt{1 + \lVert \nabla z \rVert^2}}, \qquad
\cos\theta = n_z = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \lVert \nabla z \rVert^2}}.
$$

The bed curvature is given by the Hessian $\mathbf{H} = \nabla\nabla z$, computed with second central
differences.

The flow thickness $h$ is measured **normal to the bed**, so a column holds the mass
$M = \rho h / \cos\theta$ per unit horizontal area. The velocity $\mathbf{u} = (u_x, u_y, u_z)$ is tangent to
the bed, $\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$. Its horizontal part $\mathbf{u}_h$ moves the column over the grid
and $u_z = \nabla z \cdot \mathbf{u}_h$. The speed $\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert$ is measured along the bed and is
used by all speed-dependent terms (friction, timestep, stopping, peak velocity).

The projection $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^\mathsf{T}$ removes the bed-normal component.
For a field $f$ on the horizontal grid, the surface gradient is

$$
\nabla_s f = \mathbf{P} \begin{pmatrix} \nabla f \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

A path along the bed in direction of the unit tangent $\mathbf{t}$ bends with the normal curvature

$$
\kappa = \cos\theta\ \mathbf{t}_h^\mathsf{T} \mathbf{H}\, \mathbf{t}_h,
$$

positive where the bed is concave along the path and negative where it is convex.

## Balance equations

Entrainment and deposition are not modeled, so mass only moves with the flow:

$$
\frac{\partial M}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (M \mathbf{u}_h) = 0.
$$

Depth-averaged momentum balance:

$$
\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt} =
\underbrace{-g \nabla_s z}_{\text{gravity}}
\underbrace{-\,g_n \nabla_s h}_{\text{pressure}}
\underbrace{-\,a_f \frac{\mathbf{u}}{\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert}}_{\text{friction}}
\underbrace{+\,\kappa \lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert^2 \mathbf{n}}_{\text{bed geometry}},
$$

with $\kappa$ taken in the flow direction. The gravity term is the part of gravity tangent to the bed
(magnitude $g \sin\theta$). The pressure term is hydrostatic, with the bed-normal acceleration

$$
g_n = \max\left(g\cos\theta + \kappa \lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert^2,\ 0\right)
    = \max\left((g + \mathbf{u}_h^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{H}\,\mathbf{u}_h)\cos\theta,\ 0\right),
$$

whose spatial changes are neglected. The second part is the **centripetal load**: concave terrain presses the
flow onto the bed and raises friction, convex terrain lowers it (Fischer et al. 2012). Lift-off is not
considered, so $g_n$ is clamped to zero. The centripetal load (`enable_curvature`) and the pressure term can
both be switched off.

The bed geometry term is normal to the bed: it does not change the speed but turns the velocity to stay
tangent to the curved bed. It is not evaluated explicitly but follows from projecting the velocity onto the
local tangent plane and from curved particle paths (see [advection](#grid-to-particle-transfer-and-advection)).

## Basal friction

The basal shear stress $\tau_b$ depends on the bed-normal stress

$$
\sigma_b = \rho h g_n \chi
$$

and gives $a_f = \tau_b / (\rho h)$. The factor $\chi$ is the [gully correction](#gully-confinement), one on
open terrain. Four friction laws are implemented (`friction_model`):

- **Coulomb:** $\tau_b = \mu \sigma_b$.
- **Voellmy** (Voellmy 1955):
  $\tau_b = \mu \sigma_b + \dfrac{\rho g}{\xi} \lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert^2$.
- **samosAT** (Granig & Oberndorfer 2008), a Coulomb term that increases at low speed plus a thickness-dependent
  drag:

  $$
  \tau_b = \mu \sigma_b \left(1 + \frac{R_{s,0}}{R_{s,0} + R_s}\right)
         + \frac{\rho \lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert^2}{\left(\kappa_K^{-1}\ln\max(h/\ell_r, 1) + b\right)^2},
  \qquad R_s = \frac{\rho \lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert^2}{\sigma_b},
  $$

  with $R_{s,0} = 0.222$, $\kappa_K = 0.43$, $\ell_r = 0.05$ m and $b = 4.13$.
- **$\mu(I)$ rheology** (Jop et al. 2006):

  $$
  \tau_b = \mu(I)\sigma_b, \qquad
  \mu(I) = \mu_s + \frac{\mu_2 - \mu_s}{I_0/I + 1}, \qquad
  I = \frac{5}{2}\frac{d_g \lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert}{h\sqrt{g_n \chi h}},
  $$

  with grain diameter $d_g$, using the depth-averaged shear rate $5\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert/(2h)$ of a Bagnold
  profile.

## Gully confinement

A DEM resolves gullies narrower than the flow. On a gully wall inclined by $\beta$ against the surrounding
slope, the bed-following model gives a friction per unit mass of only $\mu g_n \cos\beta$; it misses the load
that the snow in the middle of the gully puts on the walls. Operational models compensate with separate gully
friction parameters (SLF 2011). Here the missing load is derived from the stress in a filled gully.

Following Gray et al. (1999), the terrain is split into a smooth reference surface $z_{\text{ref}}$ (the DEM
smoothed with a Gaussian of standard deviation $L_{\text{ref}}$) and a basal topography. Features wider than
$L_{\text{ref}}$ are followed by the flow; narrower gullies are filled. The wall inclination is
$\cos\beta = \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{n}_{\text{ref}}$. Gravity, normal and curvature are still evaluated on the
real bed.

With a nearly flat free surface, a wall element at depth $\zeta$ carries the normal stress
$\rho g_n \zeta (\cos^2\beta + K_y \sin^2\beta)$, where $K_y = 1$ (hydrostatic, default) or the cross-slope
earth pressure coefficient of Hutter et al. (1993). Relative to the bed-following value, the normal load grows
by

$$
\chi = 1 + K_y \tan^2\beta,
$$

which is $1/\cos^2\beta$ for $K_y = 1$, the classical friction of a body pressed into a V-groove.

![Cross-section through a snow-filled gully. The reference surface z_ref (dashed) is the bed smoothed with a Gaussian filter; β is the angle between the bed normal n and the reference normal n_ref. A wall element at depth ζ carries ρ g_n ζ normal to the reference surface and K_y ρ g_n ζ across the gully, which gives the load factor χ. Not to scale.](figures/method_gully_confinement.png)

*Cross-section through a snow-filled gully. The reference surface z_ref (dashed) is the bed smoothed with a Gaussian filter; β is the angle between the bed normal n and the reference normal n_ref. A wall element at depth ζ carries ρ g_n ζ normal to the reference surface and K_y ρ g_n ζ across the gully, which gives the load factor χ. Not to scale.*

- $\beta$ is limited to $70°$ so cliffs do not produce unbounded loads.
- The Voellmy drag needs no correction, because $h$ already equals the hydraulic radius of the wetted wall.
- The correction is off for $L_{\text{ref}} < \Delta$ ($\beta = 0$, $\chi = 1$). On a plane or parabola the
  filter only adds a constant, so analytical benchmarks are unaffected.
- The derivation assumes the flow fills the cross-section and is near equilibrium across the gully; for a thin
  film on one wall the bed-following load is the better description.

## Material point discretization

The particles are the only persistent state. Each step transfers mass and momentum from particles to grid,
updates the grid velocity with the momentum balance, and moves the particles with the new grid velocity. Every
particle keeps its mass, so mass conservation holds without further treatment.

### Grid and shape functions

The MPM grid coincides with the DEM: node $i$ sits at the cell center $\mathbf{x}_i$. Particles and nodes
exchange information through quadratic B-splines,

$$
S(\eta) =
\begin{cases}
\tfrac{3}{4} - \eta^2, & |\eta| < \tfrac{1}{2},\\
\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tfrac{3}{2} - |\eta|\right)^2, & \tfrac{1}{2} \le |\eta| < \tfrac{3}{2},\\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad
w_{ip} = S(\eta_x) S(\eta_y), \qquad
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ip} = \frac{\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p}{\Delta},
$$

so each particle interacts with $3 \times 3$ nodes. The weights depend only on horizontal positions. Terrain
quantities ($\mathbf{n}$, $\cos\theta$, $\mathbf{H}$, $\cos\beta$) are computed once per node before the
simulation; at particle positions, $\mathbf{n}$, $\mathbf{H}$ and elevation are interpolated bilinearly.

### Initialization

Every release cell with slab thickness $h_0$ receives $N_{\text{ppc}}$ particles (`released_particles_per_cell`)
at rest, each with mass $m_p = \rho h_0 \Delta^2 / (\cos\theta\, N_{\text{ppc}})$, or
$m_p = \mathrm{SWE}\,\Delta^2 / N_{\text{ppc}}$ for a given snow water equivalent.

- **Stratified placement:** the cell is divided into $N_{\text{ppc}}$ equal-area boxes in
  $\mathrm{round}(\sqrt{N_{\text{ppc}}})$ rows, one random particle per box. Rows with more particles are made
  taller so every box has area $\Delta^2/N_{\text{ppc}}$ (e.g. $N_{\text{ppc}} = 13$: rows of 3, 3, 3, 4).
- **Random placement:** particles anywhere in the cell.

Filled circles: positions after relaxation, arrows show the displacement. Axes in cell sizes Δ.](figures/particle_placement_n13_corner.png)

### Particle relaxation

Either placement can be followed by a relaxation towards even spacing (default: stratified relaxed). With a
hexagonal packing of area $\Delta^2/N_{\text{ppc}}$ per particle, the target spacing is

$$
\lambda = \Delta\sqrt{\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}\,N_{\text{ppc}}}}.
$$

Each iteration pushes every particle away from neighbors closer than $\lambda$,

$$
\delta\mathbf{x}_p = \alpha \sum_{p':\,r_{pp'} < \lambda} (\lambda - r_{pp'})
\frac{\mathbf{x}_p - \mathbf{x}_{p'}}{r_{pp'}},
$$

with $\alpha = 0.3$ and $\lVert\delta\mathbf{x}_p\rVert \le \lambda/2$. Particles must stay in the release area:
if a step leaves it, only the larger component is applied, then only the smaller one, otherwise the particle
stays. After 30 iterations the particle elevations are read from the DEM. Masses do not change.

### Particle-to-grid transfer

Mass and momentum are scattered with the affine particle-in-cell (APIC) scheme (Jiang et al. 2015):

$$
m_i = \sum_p w_{ip} m_p, \qquad
m_i\mathbf{u}_i = \sum_p w_{ip} m_p (\mathbf{u}_p + \mathbf{C}_p \boldsymbol{\eta}_{ip}), \qquad
h_i = \frac{m_i \cos\theta_i}{\rho\Delta^2},
$$

where the $3 \times 2$ matrix $\mathbf{C}_p$ is the affine velocity of the particle across the horizontal grid.
Plain PIC ($\mathbf{C}_p = 0$) damps rotation, shear and lateral spreading; APIC transfers them without loss
and conserves angular momentum.

### Grid update

Particles on differently inclined parts of the bed add to the same node, so the node velocity is first
projected onto the node's tangent plane, $\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_i = \mathbf{P}_i (m_i\mathbf{u}_i)/m_i$. Then
gravity and pressure are applied,

$$
\mathbf{u}^*_i = \tilde{\mathbf{u}}_i - \Delta t\left(g(\nabla_s z)_i + g_{n,i}(\nabla_s h)_i\right),
$$

with $\nabla h$ from central differences of the nodal thicknesses. Friction is applied as a projection that
can stop a node but never reverse it,

$$
\mathbf{u}^{n+1}_i = \mathbf{u}^*_i \max\left(0,\ 1 - \frac{\Delta t\, a_f}{\lVert\mathbf{u}^*_i\rVert}\right),
$$

with $a_f$ evaluated at $\max(\lVert\mathbf{u}^*_i\rVert, v_{\text{th}})$. For Coulomb-type laws this acts as
static friction: a node at rest stays at rest while the driving acceleration is below $\mu g_n \chi$,
independent of $\Delta t$.

### Grid-to-particle transfer and advection

Particles gather the new velocity and affine matrix,

$$
\mathbf{u}_p^{n+1} = \sum_i w_{ip}\mathbf{u}_i^{n+1}, \qquad
\mathbf{C}_p^{n+1} = 4\sum_i w_{ip}\mathbf{u}_i^{n+1}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ip}^\mathsf{T},
$$

where 4 is the inverse inertia tensor of the quadratic B-spline in cell units. The particle moves with the
mean velocity $\bar{\mathbf{u}} = (\mathbf{u}_p^n + \mathbf{u}_p^{n+1})/2$ (trapezoidal rule) over the arc
length $\ell_p = \lVert\bar{\mathbf{u}}\rVert\Delta t$ in direction $\mathbf{t} = \bar{\mathbf{u}}/\lVert\bar{\mathbf{u}}\rVert$,
bending with the normal curvature $\kappa_p$:

$$
\mathbf{r}_p^{n+1} = \mathbf{r}_p^n + \left(\ell_p - \frac{\kappa_p^2\ell_p^3}{6}\right)\mathbf{t}
+ \frac{\kappa_p\ell_p^2}{2}\mathbf{n}_p, \qquad \mathbf{r}_p = (\mathbf{x}_p, z_p),
$$

the leading terms of a circular arc. The elevation is then read from the DEM so the particle stays on the bed,
and the velocity is rotated into the new tangent plane without changing its speed:

$$
\mathbf{u}_p^{n+1} \leftarrow \lVert\mathbf{u}_p^{n+1}\rVert
\frac{\mathbf{P}_p\mathbf{u}_p^{n+1}}{\lVert\mathbf{P}_p\mathbf{u}_p^{n+1}\rVert}.
$$

A particle moving onto a steeper or flatter bed thus keeps its speed and only changes direction. The curvature
still enters the normal load $g_n$.

## Time stepping and stopping

The timestep follows the CFL condition,

$$
\Delta t = \frac{C_{\text{CFL}}\,\Delta}{\max_i\left(\lVert\mathbf{u}_i\rVert + \sqrt{g h_i\cos\theta_i}\right)},
$$

over nodes holding more than 1 mm of snow, so $\Delta t$ grows again as the flow slows down.

- A particle whose speed stays below $v_{\text{th}}$ for 20 consecutive steps comes to **rest**: it keeps its
  position and mass (pressure on neighbors unchanged) but carries no momentum. It moves again above
  $2v_{\text{th}}$, so incoming flow can remobilize a deposit.
- Particles are **removed** where their stencil reaches nodes without terrain data (missing elevations, or
  within two cells of the border), and when their speed exceeds 150 m/s (numerical errors, e.g. DEM artifacts).

The simulation ends at the step limit or when

1. at least 99 % of the particles rest or are removed, or
2. fewer than four cells are newly reached within the last $\max(10, \mathrm{round}(L_w/C_{\text{CFL}}))$ steps
   (the same front travel of $L_w$ cells at any CFL number), or
3. optionally, the total momentum $\sum_i m_i\lVert\mathbf{u}_i\rVert$ falls below the fraction $\epsilon_M$ of
   its maximum so far.

## Center of mass, travel length and travel angle

The center of mass (COM) is evaluated after every step from the nodal masses:

$$
\mathbf{X}^n = \frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}} m_i^n\mathbf{x}_i}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}} m_i^n}, \qquad
Z^n = z(\mathbf{X}^n).
$$

The elevation is read from the DEM below the COM rather than mass-averaged, because the COM of a flow on a
curved bed can lie above or below the bed. By default $\mathcal{B}$ contains every node with mass. Optionally
it contains only the biggest blob (8-connected nodes with $m_i > 0$, largest total mass); the COM then stays on
the main path but jumps when another blob becomes the biggest, and the travel length grows by this jump.

Travel lengths, accumulated from the first step $n_0$ with mass:

$$
s_{\text{2D}}^n = \sum_{k=n_0+1}^{n}\lVert\mathbf{X}^k - \mathbf{X}^{k-1}\rVert, \qquad
s_{\text{3D}}^n = \sum_{k=n_0+1}^{n}\lVert(\mathbf{X}^k - \mathbf{X}^{k-1},\ Z^k - Z^{k-1})\rVert.
$$

Travel angle from the vertical drop $\Delta Z^n = Z^{n_0} - Z^n$:

$$
\alpha^n = \arctan\frac{\Delta Z^n}{s_{\text{2D}}^n}.
$$

Steps without mass or with non-finite values are skipped. $s_{\text{3D}}$ is not the straight distance between
the highest and lowest point of the footprint: the COM starts and ends inside the flow (shorter) but follows
its curves (longer), so the two are not interchangeable.

## GPU implementation

The physics runs entirely on the GPU through WebGPU, so the same code runs on any vendor's GPU, natively and in
the browser. Each MPM step is a sequence of compute shaders: grid reset, particle-to-grid transfer, grid
update, grid-to-particle transfer with advection, and an update of time, timestep and stopping state. Several
steps are recorded into one submission; the host reads back only the global state between batches.

### Fixed-point atomics

In the particle-to-grid transfer, many particles add to the same node, which needs atomic operations. Where
the hardware supports float atomics, sums are formed directly. Otherwise WebGPU only offers 32-bit integer
atomics, and contributions are rounded to fixed-point units: node mass as an unsigned integer with
$4\times10^9$ units, each momentum component as a signed integer with $\pm2\times10^9$ units. Optionally,
particles are sorted into $8\times8$-cell tiles at a fixed interval, which reduces conflicting writes.

The ranges adapt to the flow:

$$
m_{\max} = 2^{k_m} m_{\text{ref}}, \qquad (m u)_{\max} = 2^{k_q} m_{\text{ref}} u_{\text{ref}}, \qquad
m_{\text{ref}} = \rho h_{\text{ref}}\Delta^2,
$$

with $h_{\text{ref}} = 50$ m and $u_{\text{ref}} = 100$ m/s. The grid update records the largest node mass and
momentum component each step. Before the next transfer, an exponent is raised by one if this maximum fills at
least half its range and lowered by one below an eighth, so the fullest node uses between an eighth and a half
of the range. Limits: $-8 \le k_m \le 8$ and $k_m - 3 \le k_q \le 8$; the lower bound of $k_q$ keeps the
momentum range at least as large as the mass range at 12.5 m/s, since a flow starting from rest has no
momentum to measure. Before the first step, $k_m$ is set from the released particles.

For $\Delta = 5$ m, $\rho = 200$ kg/m³ and $k_m = k_q = 0$, one unit is $6\times10^{-5}$ kg of mass and
0.0125 kg m/s of momentum. Nodes so light that one momentum unit changes their velocity by more than 1 m/s are
treated as empty. The range follows the flow with a one-step delay; if a node's mass more than doubles in one
step the integer can overflow, so a fill above 90 % sets a flag in the simulation state.

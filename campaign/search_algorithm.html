The search for the general parameters

Why the calibration is a staged profile search with per-event nuisance re-fitting — and why the two procedures everyone reaches for first are unavailable and wrong, in that order.

avalanchers · baseline-calibration · companion to campaign/GLOBAL_CALIBRATION_PLAN.md and the campaign's working record

1What is being searched for

The simulator has two kinds of knob, and the whole design follows from keeping them apart. A handful are shared constants: the friction model and its optional components, the turbulent drag coefficient ξ, the numerical settings (CFL number, particles per cell, step cap), the internal friction angle. These are properties of the model, not of any particular avalanche, and they get one value for the whole world. The rest are per-event parameters: the Coulomb friction coefficient μ and the slab thickness, which plainly differ between a wet spring slide and a dry January slab and which the eventual deployed system must predict from conditions.

The measurement is the repo's own area-overlap index. For a case, both the observed outline and the simulated footprint are rasterised on the same 5 m grid; every cell in the union is classified as intersection X, missed U, or spurious O; and with α = X/T, β = U/T, γ = O/T over the union T,

ΩT  =  α − β − γ  =  2 · IoU − 1

which is −1 for disjoint footprints, 0 when the overlap is half the union, and +1 for an exact match. Where the scale sits for this project: doing nothing scores −0.696, the repo defaults score −0.390, the best single global parameter vector reaches +0.005, and calibrating every event separately reaches +0.295. That last gap — a single vector at +0.073 against per-event fitting at +0.260 on the twelve-event identifiability panel, widening rather than shrinking under leave-one-out — is the reason the architecture is per-event at all.

So the question this document is about is narrow and specific: what values should the shared constants take? Not "what parameters fit avalanche 6716" — that is the per-event problem, and it is solved. The shared constants have to be frozen first, because the 20,000-event harvest that follows produces the regression targets, and a harvest run at a badly chosen global configuration produces per-event optima that are partly compensating for that configuration. A regressor trained on those learns the compensation as if it were physics.

2Not differentiation

The instinct of anyone with a machine-learning reflex is to ask for ∂ΩT/∂θ and walk downhill. It is worth being precise about why that is unavailable here, because the reason is not the usual one ("the simulator is a black box") but something sharper.

The objective is a staircase, not a rough surface

Follow the chain in evaluate_case. Parameters go into a GPU material-point simulation; out comes a peak flow-thickness field; that field is thresholded at flow_threshold = 0.1 m into a boolean mask; the mask passes a residence gate and a connected-to-release filter, both boolean; the surviving cells are counted; and ΩT is a ratio of those integer counts. Every step after the simulation is a discretisation. Nudge μ by 10−6 and, almost always, not one cell changes side of the threshold, so ΩT is exactly unchanged. The function is piecewise constant: its true derivative is zero almost everywhere and undefined on the measure-zero set where a cell flips.

The steps are small — footprints here run 800 to 24,000 cells (2–60 ha at 5 m), so a single cell flipping moves ΩT by something like 10−4 to 10−3 — but "small steps" is not "smooth". A finite difference with a small h returns exactly 0.0, and a finite difference with a large h returns a secant across an unknown number of steps, which is a difference quotient of the discretisation and not an estimate of anything. Figure 2 shows the shape of the problem.

There is no adjoint, and building one was priced and declined

Even granting a smoothed objective, the gradient would have to come from somewhere. The options are an adjoint through the solver or finite differences, and both have been examined:

  • Adjoint / autodiff. The horizon is roughly 4,000 timesteps, about 30× beyond published practice for differentiable granular MPM, so the memory and stability cost of reverse-mode through the rollout is severe. More decisively, the particle-to-grid transfer scatters through quantised integer atomics — atomicAdd(&grid_mass_atomic[idx], u32(round(p.mass * weight * MASS_FACTOR))) in p2g.wgsl. That quantisation keeps the scatter's values exact — though not, it was later measured, the summation order: two identical 105-case runs differed on two cases by exactly one cell each (~2×10⁻⁴ in ΩT), because atomicAdd ordering varies between runs. Stage s3_roughness therefore asserts |ΔΩT| < 10⁻³ as its null check rather than bit-identity — a noise floor two orders of magnitude below the 0.02 decision band. The same quantisation makes the derivative through the transfer identically zero. Near-determinism and differentiability are bought with the same coin here, and the project spent it on the former.
  • Finite differences. A central-difference gradient in the two-dimensional inner problem costs four simulations per point, before any line search. Nelder-Mead advances a whole iteration on one or two. Direct search is not a fallback for the gradient here; it is strictly cheaper per unit of progress.

The project's own conclusion, recorded under "ruled out": making the solver differentiable is worth it only for inferring spatially varying fields — on the order of 100 to 1,000 parameters. For roughly ten scalars, derivative-free search converges in seconds to minutes.

A distinction worth keeping

It is tempting to say the objective is "noisy". It is better to say it is discontinuous and near-deterministic. Re-running the same parameters returns the same score to within ~2×10⁻⁴ (a one-cell flip from atomic summation order, on 2 of 105 cases) — stochasticity two orders of magnitude below the 0.02 decision band. What defeats differentiation is the staircase, not that residual flicker — and that matters, because the standard remedies for noise (averaging replicates, stochastic gradients) buy nothing against a staircase.

3Not per-avalanche-best-then-average

The second instinct is more sophisticated and much more dangerous, because it produces a number that looks perfectly reasonable. It goes: we already fit every event separately, so for a shared constant like ξ, take each event's fitted ξ*, average them, and freeze that. One line of pandas. It is wrong, and the project has measured how wrong.

ξ is not identified

The evidence is a 12×12 grid of ΩT over (μ, ξ) for twelve events — 1,728 simulations. For each event, take the set of grid points scoring within 0.02 of that event's best (0.02 being the band at which this project declines to distinguish two parameter vectors at all) and ask how far each axis can travel inside it:

The cleanest single illustration is aval_6716, whose admissible ξ window spans 13.5× against 1.33× for μ. The grid scan puts its optimum at ξ = 12000 with ΩT = 0.2044; Nelder-Mead, on the same event, reports ξ = 2605 with ΩT = 0.2077. A 4.6× difference in the parameter, 0.003 in the score — and the lower ξ scores very slightly better, so you cannot even say the grid was closer to the truth. Neither number is that event's "best ξ". Both are arbitrary points on a level set, and which one you get depends on the optimiser, not on the avalanche.

The near-optimal set is a diagonal ridge, and that is the whole story

The within-tolerance region is not a blob. Fitting a principal axis to it, in coordinates where μ and log ξ are each normalised to their search range, gives an axial mean orientation of +65° at the 0.02 band (alignment R = 0.71; at the 0.05 band +69°, R = 0.95), with elongation growing from 1.6 to 3.4 as the band widens from 0.02 to 0.10. The ridge is steep — long in log ξ, narrow in μ — which is the geometric statement of "ξ is not identified, μ is".

The positive slope has a physical reading, and it is the one you would predict. Larger ξ means weaker turbulent drag, so the flow is more mobile; to arrest it at the same observed runout you need more Coulomb friction. The optimal μ is a function of ξ. What the data constrain is the pair, not either coordinate alone.

Two separate failures, and only the second is fatal

First, averaging arbitrary points is meaningless. If each event's reported ξ* is wherever its optimiser stopped along a near-level set, then the average of the ξ* is an average of optimiser path-dependence. Widen the tolerance a little and the numbers would all change without a single score changing meaningfully. A statistic whose value is set by the stopping rule is not an estimate.

Second, and worse: each event's fitted μ was co-adapted to that event's arbitrary ξ. Reuse that μ and impose a shared ξ afterwards, and you have moved along the vertical in Figure 1B — off the ridge, into a region worse than either end of it. This is not a hypothetical. It was run:

The same ξ costs 3% of the achievable per-event gain when it is fixed before calibration and 52% when it is pinned afterwards. The parameter did not change. The order did. That factor of seventeen is the entire justification for the algorithm in §4.

Stated as a principle, so it transfers: you cannot average conditional optima of an ill-identified parameter. The average of the argmaxes is not the argmax of the average, and when the argmax is nearly arbitrary along a level set the average is nearly meaningless — while the companion parameters that were fitted alongside it carry the arbitrariness forward into whatever you do next.

Two honest qualifications. The 52% figure was measured by pinning ξ to 754 specifically, not by averaging the twelve fitted ξ and pinning that; averaging has not been separately measured. The failure mechanism is co-adaptation, which does not care which shared value you land on, so there is no reason to expect the average to be rescued — but the arithmetic of "average then pin" is an inference here, not a measurement. Separately, the per-event optima do not all sit on one common ridge: across 105 events, corr(μ*, log ξ*) is only +0.20. Each event has its own ridge, roughly parallel to the others but offset — which is exactly what Figure 1 draws, and it is the good outcome, since it means the per-event optima carry event-specific information rather than tracing one shared curve.

4What the search actually is

The procedure has a name in statistics, and using it makes the design legible: a staged profile search over the shared constants, with the per-event parameters treated as nuisance parameters and re-optimised inside every comparison. Five ingredients, each doing one job.

4.1   The profile objective

Write θ for a candidate vector of shared constants and φe = (μ, slab) for the per-event parameters of case e. The merit of θ is

Ω̄(θ)  =  (1/N)  ∑e   maxφe   ΩT(θ, φe)

The inner maximisation is the point. It is the direct analogue of the profile likelihood Lp(θ) = maxη L(θ, η), in which the parameter of interest is scanned and the nuisance parameters η are re-maximised — "profiled out" — at every candidate value, rather than held at some previously fitted value. Holding them fixed is precisely the mistake of Figure 1B; profiling them is Figure 1C.

μ and slab are nuisance parameters only for this question. They are the project's eventual regression targets and the most interesting quantities in it. But when the question is "which ξ should be frozen", they are things that must be allowed to move so they cannot contaminate the answer.

4.2   The inner solver: Nelder-Mead

The inner problem is two-dimensional, bounded, cheap, and — with ξ held — well behaved. The harness solves it with a bounded Nelder-Mead simplex in the unit cube, which is derivative-free by construction:

  • Parameters are mapped to [0,1] per dimension (log for ξ and roughness, linear otherwise), and the simplex is built from the start point plus one vertex per free dimension displaced by 0.25 — three vertices for a 2-D inner problem.
  • Standard moves: reflect at 1.0, expand at 2.0, contract at −0.5, shrink toward the best vertex at 0.5, with every proposal clamped to the box.
  • It stops when the simplex's Chebyshev radius drops below 0.01, or at the evaluation budget. A simulation that fails returns +2.0, worse than any attainable score, so failures push the simplex away rather than crashing the run.

The budget is 40 evaluations, and that number is measured rather than guessed: on exactly this configuration the fixed-ξ run used a mean of 34.2 and a median of 35 evaluations, with 81 of 105 searches terminating by simplex collapse rather than budget exhaustion. The inner loop is not being truncated; it is converging.

4.3   A fixed panel and paired comparison

Every candidate is scored on the same 102 cases — the 105-case set minus aval_4117, aval_7743 and aval_847, whose fabricated-terrain contamination overlaps a footprint by ≥1% — with the same inner start (μ = 0.36, slab = 0.56, the panel medians of the fixed-ξ optima) and therefore the identical initial simplex. Candidates are compared case-by-case, and the statistic is the paired mean difference.

This is the single largest variance reduction in the design, and it is worth seeing the size of it. Between events, ΩT at the per-event optimum has a standard deviation of 0.224 (measured across the 105 fixed-ξ optima: they range from −0.19 to +0.69). The paired difference between two nearby candidates on the same event is expected to have a standard deviation near 0.10, because the inner loop absorbs most of what distinguishes them. At n = 102 that is a standard error near 0.0099, so the 0.02 decision band is roughly a 2σ effect. An unpaired two-sample comparison at σ = 0.224 would need about 1,024 cases per arm for the same standard error. Pairing buys a factor of ten in panel size, for free, by asking every candidate to face identical data.

One deliberate refusal: the inner loop is not warm-started from each case's own previous optimum, even though that would be cheaper. Warm-starting would advantage configurations near the incumbent, which is the one bias this experiment cannot afford.

4.4   Bootstrap confidence intervals and an explicit freeze rule

A challenger replaces the incumbent only if both the paired mean ΔΩT is at least 0.02 and the 95% paired bootstrap confidence interval excludes zero. Otherwise the incumbent stands, and ties inside the band break toward lower cost and then toward fewer enabled components. The 0.02 is not arbitrary: it is the same band at which the identifiability work declines to distinguish two parameter vectors, so a global knob that moves the panel mean by less than that is not worth freezing to a non-default value.

The bootstrap, rather than a t-interval, because the paired differences are bounded, skewed and lumpy — many are exactly zero, on cases where the footprint does not change at all — and resampling cases makes no distributional assumption about them. The rule is applied by analyze_stage.py, which prints a verdict rather than a table, so the decision cannot drift with the reader.

4.5   Greedy stagewise sequencing

The stages run in a fixed order, each freezing one tier and passing its winner forward — numerics, then discrete structure, then the ξ profile, then the remaining constants, then a confirmation pass.

Two details of the ladder matter for understanding the method rather than the project. Stage 0 is the one stage with no inner loop: numerics are evaluated once per case at that case's own already-fitted optimum, because the question there is "does this setting change the score at the operating point the harvest will run at", not "does it move the optimum". And stage 2's decision rule is deliberately not the argmax — since ξ is the parameter known not to be identified, the profile will be flat and its argmax will be noise, so the rule is to take the region within 0.01 of the maximum and choose the geometric centre of that plateau, recording the plateau's width as the honest statement of how well ξ is determined at panel level.

5Why it works

It asks the deployment question, not a proxy for it

At deployment, θ is frozen in the binary and φe arrives per event from the regressor or mixture density network. So the correct merit for a candidate θ is: when every event is allowed to adapt its own conditional knobs, which shared value serves the panel best? That is Ω̄(θ) verbatim. Any scoring that holds φe at stale values is measuring a situation that will never occur.

It is robust to the ridge by construction

The ridge says the pair (μ, ξ) is what the data constrain. The profile respects that: pick the shared coordinate first, and let each event slide along its own ridge to the conditional μ that suits it at that ξ. Nothing is ever asked of ξ that ξ alone can answer. This is why non-identifiability, which is fatal to the averaging approach, is merely an inconvenience here — it makes the profile flat, and a flat profile is a result you can report (the plateau width) rather than a failure.

The empirical proof is already in hand. "Fix ξ, then calibrate μ and slab at that ξ" is exactly the inner loop, and it was measured at 97% of the free-ξ oracle. Stage 2 is that same procedure run at nine values of ξ instead of one. The method is not being introduced on faith; it is being scaled from a measurement.

The side effects were measured too, and they all point the same way

  • The inner search converges. With ξ free, 11 of 105 searches terminated by convergence; with ξ fixed, 81 of 105 do on the same budget. The optimiser had been spending its evaluations wandering the non-identified direction.
  • The targets get more predictable. Correlations with the fitted slab all rise once ξ is fixed: size class +0.36 → +0.44, area +0.28 → +0.37, frac_wdh +0.30 → +0.34 — and that last one is a deployable feature.
  • A known leak shrinks. The correlation between fitted μ and aval_shape — outline digitisation quality, a property of the mapping rather than of the avalanche — falls from +0.31 to +0.25.

Removing a badly identified degree of freedom did not cost accuracy; it bought convergence, cleaner regression targets, and a smaller leak, for 3% of the score.

6Why it is the most efficient option available

Cost here is counted in simulations. The hardware retires them at 90–180 per second aggregate on the eight-GPU box — 0.3 to 0.65 million per hour — with 64 workers running concurrently; a single RTX 5090 was measured at 23.4 cases/s. The whole 322,300-simulation experiment fits in 30 to 60 minutes. That economic fact should shape the algorithm, and it does.

  • Derivative-free inner solve exploits an inner problem that is 2-D, bounded and benign: about 34 simulations per (candidate, case), with four out of five searches converging rather than exhausting.
  • Profiling one shared coordinate at a time exploits the low effective dimensionality of the shared block. Most of the ten nominal dimensions are not live: density cancels analytically, roughness is unread on every code path this harness exercises, and entrainment was selected in 1 of 104 free searches.
  • Greedy staging replaces an exponential factorial with a short sequence of cheap decisions. The full factorial over stages 0–3 is 75 × 48 × 9 × 7 = 226,800 configurations; at 102 cases and 40 evaluations each that is roughly 9.3×108 simulations, about 60 days of box time at the optimistic throughput. The staged ladder answers the same questions in 322,300 simulations — a factor of nearly 3,000.
  • Pairing on a fixed panel shrinks the variance of every comparison, which is what makes a 0.02 difference decidable with 102 cases instead of a thousand.
  • Cheap parallel evaluations invert the usual trade. When a function evaluation is a millisecond-scale GPU job and 64 run at once, a brute inner loop is cheaper than clever outer machinery that spends serial thinking time to save evaluations.

Against the obvious alternatives

CMA-ES. The right tool for the later per-event work — it is what the upstream thesis plans for exactly that, and it is what you would want if the inner problem were seven-dimensional and multimodal. For profiling one or two shared coordinates it is a population method with a covariance matrix to adapt, needing hundreds of evaluations before the adaptation earns its keep, applied to a question that nine grid points answer exactly. Use it where the dimension justifies it, which is not here.

Bayesian optimisation. Its premium is sample efficiency, bought by spending modelling effort per evaluation and by serialising — each proposal wants the previous result. That trade pays when evaluations are scarce and expensive. Here they are abundant, cheap and embarrassingly parallel, so the surrogate's savings are denominated in the resource that is not scarce while its serialisation taxes the one that is. Its usual surrogate also assumes a smoothness the staircase objective of §2 does not have.

Full grid search. The standard complaint against grids is dimensional: cost grows exponentially. Profiling removes the dimension, and at dimension one a grid is the efficient choice — it is exhaustive over the range, trivially parallel, and it returns the whole profile rather than a point, which is what the plateau rule needs. That is why stages 2 and 3 are grids: nine ξ values, seven angles. The method here is not "avoid grid search"; it is "reduce to a dimension where grid search is correct".

7What the design does not claim

Three limitations are structural and are handled explicitly rather than hidden.

Single-start Nelder-Mead is not a global optimiser. A candidate could in principle lose because its basin is awkward from the shared start rather than because it is worse. Stage 4 re-runs the finalists from three different starts and pools the best per case; if the ranking is start-sensitive, that is where it shows. This is a check, not a proof.

Greedy ordering is a real risk. Stage 1 screens 48 structures at ξ = 754 — the value the +0.2864 run used, deliberately not the repo default of 4000, which sits in the least dissipative corner of the published RAMMS range and would reward whichever structure best compensates for a bad ξ. A structure that is only best at 754 would still survive stage 1. Stage 4 re-runs the top four at the frozen ξ to catch it. Bought off, not eliminated.

The panel is one storm. All 18,737 SPOT6 polygons are from 24 January 2018, so every calibrated event shares a snowpack. What gets frozen is the general configuration for this snowpack, and the honest reading of any result downstream is "given this snowpack, what does terrain predict?" That is narrower than the question the target architecture poses.

And one piece of arithmetic to verify rather than assume: the power calculation in §4.3 rests on the paired standard deviation actually being near 0.10. Stage 0 produces exactly such paired comparisons, so the realised value is computed from the stage-0 rows before stage 1 is read. If it comes in much higher, the panel cannot resolve 0.02 and the band must widen — which is a finding about the experiment, not a reason to quietly lower the bar.

8Glossary

ΩT
The area-overlap score used throughout: α − β − γ over the union of observed and simulated footprints, equivalently 2 · IoU − 1. Range −1 (disjoint) to +1 (exact). Computed from integer cell counts, which is why it is piecewise constant in the parameters.
Nuisance parameter
A parameter that must be in the model for it to be correct, but whose value is not the question being asked. Here μ and slab are nuisance parameters when choosing ξ — even though they are, in the wider project, the quantities of primary interest. The defining property is that a nuisance parameter must be re-optimised at every candidate value of the parameter of interest, never held at a previously fitted value.
Profile likelihood
The technique of eliminating nuisance parameters by maximising over them: Lp(θ) = maxη L(θ, η). The parameter of interest is scanned across a range and the nuisance parameters are re-fitted at each point, producing a curve in θ alone. The analogue here replaces the likelihood with mean ΩT and the re-fit with a Nelder-Mead search, but the logic — and the reason it is right when parameters are correlated — is identical.
Nelder-Mead simplex
A derivative-free direct-search method. In n dimensions it maintains n+1 points, and at each iteration reflects the worst point through the centroid of the rest, expanding if that helps a lot, contracting if it does not help, and shrinking the whole simplex toward the best point if nothing works. It needs only function values, never gradients, which is what makes it usable on a staircase objective.
Paired comparison
Evaluating every candidate on the identical set of cases and comparing case-by-case differences, rather than comparing group means. Between-case variation cancels in the difference, so the variance of the comparison collapses — here, from a between-event standard deviation of 0.224 to a paired standard deviation near 0.10, which is worth roughly a factor of ten in the number of cases needed.
Bootstrap confidence interval
An interval built by resampling the observed cases with replacement many times, recomputing the statistic on each resample, and taking percentiles of the resulting distribution. It makes no assumption that the differences are normally distributed, which matters here because paired ΩT differences are bounded, skewed and contain many exact zeros.
Greedy / stagewise search
Deciding one group of parameters at a time and passing the winner forward, instead of searching all combinations. It trades a guarantee for a cost reduction — the risk is that a choice made early is only best under conditions that later stages change — and the standard mitigation, used here, is a confirmation pass that re-runs the leading candidates under the final frozen configuration.

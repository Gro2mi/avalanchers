Scoring the runout model against 105 observed avalanche outlines

A hundred and five mapped avalanches from the 24 January 2018 Swiss Alps cycle, simulated on swissALTI3D terrain at 5 m and scored with compute_core::evaluation. With the parameters the repository ships, the model reaches almost everything the real avalanches reached and then keeps going, covering a median 3.2× the observed area. Adding real snowpack forcing — storm-slab depth and density measured at 96 IMIS stations — helps, though not as much as fitting the friction parameters do on the area index — but it is clearly better on runout distance, which §5 argues is the metric to believe.

What to take away
  • The shipped defaults add real skill, and still overshoot badly. They score mean ΩT = -0.390 against a do-nothing null of -0.696 — a margin of +0.306, so the flow model is doing something. But the simulated footprint is a median 3.2× the observed one, and the model runs +357 m too far — and that is a floor, not an estimate: 102/105 default runs hit the domain edge, and on a domain twice as wide the same figure is +632 m (§10). β ≈ 0.01: it reaches nearly everything that was hit, it just does not stop.
  • Measured snowpack helps — and the area index understates it. Replacing the 1.0 m uniform slab with the storm-slab depth and density actually measured at the IMIS stations — and fitting nothing at all — moves the mean to -0.142, a gain of +0.248 over the repo defaults. Calibrating μ, ξ and one amplitude on top adds +0.149 more. And the area index understates it: on runout distance the measured field overruns by +56 m against +138 m for an equally-scoring fitted uniform slab, on a median observed reach of 626 m.
  • The overshoot is not mainly lateral — it is in every direction at once. The model runs 1.53× too far and 1.65× too wide (ratio 1.08), with only 55 of 105 cases proportionally wider than long. So a fix aimed only at lateral spreading will not recover the area, and the length number is a lower bound because 102/105 runs hit the domain edge (§10).
  • Per-event optima do not cluster. Calibrating each avalanche separately lifts the mean from -0.390 to +0.294 — a +0.685 skill gap that a single global vector leaves on the table. But size class explains only 10% of the variance in the per-event μ. The exposé's plan to train a mixture density network on event characteristics has very little signal to learn from, at least on this axis.
  • Velocities are still the thing not to trust. Peak velocities at the calibrated optimum collapse to 9 m/s median (0–49) where size 3–4 avalanches run 20–50 m/s. Extent is being matched by throttling the flow, which is the single most important caveat in this report.

Isolation is now "no other mapped polygon within 25 m of this one's bounding box", relaxed from 100 m. On 24 Jan 2018 many paths released repeatedly and overlap; where they do, "the observed extent" is ambiguous. 25 m keeps the reference unambiguous while admitting 602 candidates. The per-case neighbour distance is recorded so the sensitivity can be checked later.

Stratification

Selecting 105 of 602 is still a degree of freedom, so the sample is drawn by round-robin over strata rather than by ranking on anything. The primary axis is outline quality (aval_shape): quality 1 — exact outlines — is deliberately over-sampled to 50% of the sample against 33% of the dataset, because it is the only fully trustworthy ground truth: a score against a partly-interpolated outline is a weaker measurement than one against a mapped edge. Inside each quality band the draw round-robins over size class × aspect octant × drop-height tercile, so no stratum can dominate. Everything is seeded and reproducible.

The resulting sample spans 2.0–51.6 ha (median 7.0), drop heights 154–1004 m (median 334), and start zones 1636–3926 m. No size-2 avalanche survives the 2 ha area floor, and the dataset contains exactly one eligible size-5, so the size axis is effectively 3 vs 4.

Split before tuning. Every case was assigned to calibration (66) or held out (39) by a deterministic hash of its feature index, written into cases100.json before any simulation was run. Every search in this report fits on the calibration split only; every evaluation is reported on both.

Failures

None at the headline settings. All 105 cases prepared and ran. One case does fail at the wider 600 m padding used for the domain-edge check in §10 — aval_3376, whose enlarged footprint reaches a gap in swissALTI3D coverage and returns a DEM containing NaN. That run therefore has 104 cases, not 105, and the harness records the reason rather than dropping it silently. That is worth stating explicitly, because it was not free: the swissALTI3D fetch failed on every uncached tile until the cause was found (see §10). Two earlier attempts lost 60–90 % of the sample to what looked like missing coverage and was actually a broken name lookup on this machine. A silently shrinking sample is the failure mode to watch for here.

2 · Method

Terrain, rasterisation and scoring

Each polygon is rasterised at 5 m by RasterGrid::from_polygon, padded by 300 m, and that padded footprint is the BBox handed to TileManager, which downloads swissALTI3D tiles, block-mean resamples them to 5 m and caches them in a zarr store (67 MB for all 105 cases). The unpadded polygon is then rasterised onto exactly the DEM grid, so observation and simulation live on identical cells.

A cell counts as affected if its peak flow thickness ≥ 0.1 m. evaluate_mass_movement_area normalises α, β, γ by the union, so α+β+γ = 1 and

Ω_T = α − β − γ = 2α − 1 = 2·IoU − 1

ΩT is a rescaled Jaccard index: 0.0 means IoU 0.50, +0.3 means IoU 0.65, −0.5 means IoU 0.25. It is Heiser et al. (2017).

Release areas, and the null they imply

The dataset maps total avalanche outlines, not release zones, so the release area is constructed: cells inside the observed polygon whose elevation is in the top 25 % of the polygon's elevation range and whose Horn slope is 28–60°. That is close to the exposé's specified rule (upper 20 %, Korzeniowska et al. 2017); the release-band sweep in §8 shows what the choice is worth.

This still inflates every absolute score. The release area is a subset of the reference polygon, so a model handed it that then moves nothing at all already scores -0.696 rather than −1. Every table below reports that null in the same row, and the difference is the meaningful quantity. The band fraction is held fixed at 0.25 in all searches: letting the optimiser move it buys score by painting the reference polygon, not by flowing.

New in this pass: a release thickness field

slab_thickness was one scalar for the whole domain, doing double duty as released volume and as drag scaling (the turbulent term goes as 1/d). It is now optionally a field:

d(x) = amp · HN(z) · f_slope(θ) · (1 + wind · cos(aspect(x) − lee))

with HN(z) the storm-slab depth measured at the IMIS stations and interpolated to this avalanche (§5), f_slope the Swiss-guideline slab thinning on steep terrain (Salm, Burkard & Gubler 1990, normalised to 1 at 28°), and the last factor a crude wind-loading term. Note the engine already divides the released volume by cos θ (initialize_particles.wgsl:30), so d is correctly the slope-normal thickness and the two corrections do not double-count.

New in this pass: runout length and width, separately

Area alone cannot distinguish "runs too far" from "spreads too wide". Each case now defines a path axis from the release apex to the lowest cell of the observed outline, and both the observed and simulated footprints are projected onto it: runout length is the maximum projection along the axis, width is twice the 90th percentile of the perpendicular offset. Both are reported per case, in metres. §6 is what they say.

The axis is straight, so on a sinuous path this understates length and overstates width. Both footprints are projected onto the same axis, defined by the observed outline only, so the bias is common to numerator and denominator and the ratios reported in §6 are far more robust than the absolute metres. The 90th percentile rather than the maximum keeps a single stray cell from setting the width.

3 · How the score works, and why it is not an RMSE

Everything below is scored with ΩT, the index compute_core::evaluation already computes and the one the thesis will report. It is worth three paragraphs, because it is not a familiar quantity and it is easy to misread.

What it measures

Both the observation and the simulation are binary masks on the same 5 m grid — each cell was either affected or it was not. Every cell in the union of the two falls into exactly one of three buckets, and each is expressed as a fraction of that union:

α  hit       cell affected in both              (the model got it right)
β  miss      observed, not simulated           (the model failed to reach it)
γ  overshoot simulated, not observed           (the model went somewhere it should not)

α + β + γ = 1        Ω_T = α − β − γ

So ΩT is hits minus mistakes, on a scale where the two kinds of mistake cost the same. Because the three shares sum to one, the definition collapses to something more familiar:

Ω_T = 2α − 1 = 2·IoU − 1

It is the Jaccard index (intersection over union) rescaled from [0, 1] to [−1, 1]. Nothing more. Every table below therefore prints the IoU next to it, and you can convert either way in your head: −1 disjoint footprints, −0.5 IoU 0.25, 0.0 the overlap equals half the union, +0.3 IoU 0.65, +1 exact agreement.

Why not squared error

The natural instinct is to ask for an RMSE, and it does not survive contact with the data. There is no magnitude to square: a cell is affected or it is not, so the per-cell error is 0 or 1 and the mean square error degenerates into a count of disagreeing cells. That count is not comparable between cases — a 200 m error around a 7 ha avalanche is a catastrophe, the same 200 m around a 50 ha one is a detail — and it has no upper bound, so a mean over 105 avalanches is dominated by whichever ones happened to be biggest. Normalising by the union is exactly what fixes that, and once you normalise by the union you have the Jaccard index. There is also no natural way for a squared error to price a miss against an overshoot; ΩT at least states its exchange rate explicitly (one to one) so you can argue with it.

Where a metric in metres is meaningful, this report gives one: the runout-distance error, the straight-line distance from the release apex to the furthest cell the model affected minus the same distance for the observed outline. That is a real length, it is signed, and it is the number an engineer siting a building would actually reason about. It is reported per configuration alongside ΩT, and it is computed in the harness for all 105 cases, not derived from the plotted subset.

Two things it is not. It is a straight line from the apex, not a distance along the path, so on a curved or channelised track it understates how far the flow actually travelled — for the observation and the simulation alike, which is why the difference is more trustworthy than either absolute number. And at the repository defaults it is clipped by the domain: 102 of 105 default runs reach the edge of the 300 m padded box, so the model would have kept going if allowed and the reported +357 m is a lower bound. §10 repeats the measurement at 600 m padding to show how much is hidden; the calibrated runs stop well inside the domain (30/105 clipped) and barely move.

The floor every number sits on

The release area is constructed inside the observed polygon (§2), so it is handed to the model as a subset of the right answer. A model that takes that release area and then moves nothing at all therefore does not score −1; it scores -0.696, because the release cells alone already overlap the observation. That is the do-nothing null, and it is the zero point that matters:

Read every configuration below the same way: the absolute ΩT is inflated by the release area (§3), the margin over the null in the same row is not.

Three things are worth reading off this table. Outline quality barely matters: exact outlines score +0.001 and partially-estimated ones -0.012, a difference of +0.013 against a standard error of ±0.039. The storm

The avalanche cycle was driven by the 20–23 January 2018 storm; the SPOT6 image is the 24th. Across 96 stations the summed daily new snow was a median 1.19 m, and the snow depth actually gained over the storm — the storm layer still present on the 23rd — a median 0.47 m, a settlement ratio of 0.39. Mean air temperature over the storm was -3.4 °C and the wind blew from 222° (SW), so lee slopes face 42° (NE).

The honest negative result first. Elevation is a bad predictor of how much snow this storm left: R² = 0.023 on the storm-layer depth, and only 0.059 on the raw new-snow sum. The gradient is real but tiny (+1.3 cm/100 m, t = 1.5); between-station scatter of ±0.19 m swamps it. A pure "new snow increases with elevation" field — the obvious first idea — would therefore have been almost worthless. So each avalanche instead gets a local estimate: detrended inverse-distance weighting of the six nearest stations, each lifted to the release elevation with the fitted lapse rate before averaging. Depth and mass are interpolated separately and the density taken as their ratio, which conserves the observed storm mass.

The result is a per-case storm slab of 0.11–0.99 m (median 0.58) at a density of 185–450 kg/m³ (median 289), with the nearest station between 0.4 and 16.7 km away. Before any terrain correction that is already a big change from the repo's uniform 1.0 m at 200 kg/m³.

Did it help?

"Zero free parameters" means nothing was fitted to the observed outlines: μ and ξ sit at the repository defaults and the slab depth and density come from the stations. The release construction (upper 25 % of the elevation range, 28–60°) and the 0.1 m affected-area threshold are shared by every row, so they cancel out of the comparison rather than being free.

Yes, though fitting still does more. The measured field with nothing fitted to these outlines scores -0.142 — +0.248 on the repository defaults — while fitting μ, ξ and a uniform slab thickness reaches +0.005 (+0.396). Measurement gets most of the way there for free, but it does not replace calibration. Density contributes nothing at all: swapping the repository's 200 kg/m³ for the measured 307 while leaving the slab at 1.0 m moves the score to -0.390 — the extra mass is almost exactly compensated by the extra drag it implies, so the whole effect is in the depth. A uniform slab at the sample-median measured depth reaches -0.261, so +0.119 of the full field's advantage comes from the spatial part rather than from simply using a thinner slab. That spatial part splits further: giving each avalanche its own interpolated depth but no terrain correction gets -0.254 (+0.007 on the uniform median), and adding the Salm slope-thinning term on top is worth a further +0.112. So essentially all of it is the terrain correction, not the weather. Giving each avalanche its own interpolated storm depth is worth almost nothing over just using the sample median — which is the same story the R² told: this storm's spatial pattern is not resolvable from a station network at avalanche scale. What pays is knowing that slabs thin on steep ground. Fitting μ, ξ and one amplitude on top of the field reaches +0.007 (-0.001 held out). The fitted amplitude is 1.10, i.e. the optimiser wants 10% more snow than the stations measured — close enough to 1 that the measured depth is doing the work rather than being overridden. Wind loading adds -0.001 on top of that, which is within noise — unsurprising given that the loading direction is a single country-wide number.

The two metrics disagree here, and the runout distance is the one to believe. By area index the measured field is worth +0.002 over a plain fitted uniform slab (+0.007 vs +0.005) — indistinguishable. By runout distance it is not close: the fitted uniform slab still overruns the observed reach by a median +138 m (+22% of the 626 m median observed reach), the measured field by +56 m (+9%), and the median absolute error falls from 226 m to 175 m. ΩT cannot see this, because a footprint that is slightly too long and slightly too narrow scores the same as one that is the right length and the right width. For anything decision-relevant — where does the avalanche stop, what does it hit — the physically grounded slab is the better model by a factor of 2.5 on the error that matters, and the area index would have led you to call it a tie.

A 1-D snowpack scheme?

The brief allowed for a settlement/densification model to estimate slab depth and density at release. I did not write one, and the reason is in the numbers above: the station data already measures both, four days after the storm began and one day before the image. ΔHS is the settled depth and ΔSWE/ΔHS its density — those are the quantities an Anderson-style settlement law would have been trying to predict, and predicting them from HN24 and temperature would have been strictly worse than reading them off the instrument. The one thing a snowpack model would add that this does not is the vertical structure (where the failure layer sits), which the runout model has no way to use.

The load-bearing caveat on all of this. ΔHS at a flat, wind-sheltered station is not the slab that released on a 35° slope 5 km away. It understates the new layer (the old pack settles underneath it, so ρ = ΔSWE/ΔHS is correspondingly an over-estimate), it says nothing about wind redistribution into the actual start zone, and IMIS stations sit at 1970–2953 m while some of these release zones are higher. What is defensible is the mass ΔSWE, which is measured directly and is what drives the dynamics; the split of that mass into depth and density is the soft part.

Neither direction dominates. The model overshoots the runout length by a median 1.53× and the width by 1.65× — a ratio of 1.08, i.e. it is close to equally wrong in both directions. And the length figure is a lower bound: 102 of 105 default runs reach the padded domain edge, so the model would have run further if the domain had let it (§10 quantifies this at 600 m padding). The calibrated model brings both close to observed — 1.08× long and 1.08× wide — so whatever the mechanism, calibration suppresses it in both directions rather than selectively. What survives cleanly is the joint statement: area is out by 3.2×, and that is roughly 1.53 × 1.65 of error compounding, so neither dimension can be ignored and a fix that only shortens the runout will not recover the area.

The same measurement on a domain that clips less. At 300 m padding 102 of 105 default runs hit the boundary, which truncates the length error specifically. Repeating the decomposition at 600 m padding (79/104 still clipped) gives 1.89× too long and 2.23× too wide — a lateral ratio of 1.18 against 1.08 at 300 m. So the lateral bias is real and slightly larger than the headline table suggests, but it is still a tilt of under a quarter rather than a dominant lateral failure. Both errors grow when the domain stops hiding them: the model is simply too mobile in every direction.
Where to look in the code — with the evidence it actually has. grid_physics.wgsl:103 computes a lateral_factor that gates the earth-pressure force on the surface gradient exceeding the internal friction angle — a yield criterion that would stop a thin flow from spreading indefinitely — and then never uses it; the force is applied unconditionally. That remains a real bug and an obvious experiment. But the measurement above does not single it out: the model overshoots length and width in nearly equal proportion, which is what excess mobility looks like in general, not specifically what an ungated lateral force looks like. Switching lateral_factor on is still worth doing — it is a few lines and the width metric now exists to score it — but the honest prior is that it explains part of the overshoot, not most of it.

7 · Per-event versus global calibration — and whether an MDN has anything to learn

The exposé's actual plan is to calibrate per avalanche (CMA-ES) and then train a mixture density network to predict parameters from event characteristics. At 94 ms a forward run, per-event calibration is now cheap enough to just do: 105 avalanches × 45 Nelder-Mead evaluations each, bounded, free in (μ, ξ, d). That answers two questions directly.

This is a genuinely useful negative result for the thesis. Size class explains 10% of the variance in the per-event μ, outline quality 10%, aspect octant 8%. The problem is the size of it: the best predictor available leaves 90% of the variation in the per-event optimum unexplained. A mixture density network trained on these characteristics would have very little to learn, and the honest version of the exposé's RQ is therefore: find event descriptors that do carry signal (start-zone curvature, channelisation, forest cover, local wind exposure are the obvious candidates — none of them are in the DBF) rather than assume size and aspect will do. Worth saying plainly before ~25 000 avalanches are put through a CMA-ES.

One measurement check, because the objective is jagged and a 45-evaluation Nelder-Mead on a single case fits a noisier surface than the same budget on 66. The spread of the top-decile evaluations is recorded per case in perevent100.json: the median spread in μ is 0.015, against a between-case standard deviation of 0.138. So search noise is only about 11% of the observed scatter — the per-event optima really do differ from avalanche to avalanche. That makes the negative result sharper rather than softer: the variation is genuine, it is simply not predictable from size, aspect, quality, drop height, area or start-zone elevation.

All searches use bounded Nelder-Mead in a unit cube (ξ on a log axis) on the 66 calibration cases only. Bounds: μ ∈ [0.05, 0.60], ξ ∈ [200, 12000], slab ∈ [0.10, 2.00] m, amplitude ∈ [0.05, 2.00], wind ∈ [0, 0.90].

  • μ has a clear interior optimum near 0.34, and the surface spans -0.133 to +0.006 across the swept range, so it is well identified — unusually so for a Voellmy friction coefficient.
  • ξ is weakly identified: the whole 200–8000 range spans 0.112 in ΩT, best at 2000. Reporting it to more than one significant figure would be false precision.
  • The storm-slab amplitude peaks at 1.1 — i.e. the optimiser wants more snow than the stations measured. It is the strongest single knob, spanning 0.332.
  • Density matters and the measured value is near-optimal: best at 120 kg/m³, spanning 0.000. The repo's 200 is not where the data puts it.
  • The affected-area threshold is not a free lunch. Best 0.1 m; 0.1–0.2 m is a plateau; below it a thin particle veneer inflates the footprint, above 0.4 m the deposits are shredded and the score collapses.
  • The release band still rises monotonically — the degeneracy from §2, which is why it is frozen at 0.25 in every search.
  • Wind loading does almost nothing: the entire 0–0.9 range spans 0.211, best at 0. One country-wide lee direction is too coarse to help.

10 · Diagnostics, and what broke

The domain edge hides overshoot

102 of 105 default runs reach the edge of the 300 m padded domain, so the reported γ is optimistic — the real overshoot is larger than measured. Quantifying it:

Doubling the padding moves the default score from -0.390 to -0.503 and the area ratio from 2.73× to 3.41×, with 79/104 runs still touching the edge. The runout error says it more directly: +357 m at 300 m padding becomes +632 m at 600 m, so roughly 275 m of the defaults' overshoot is simply being hidden by the box. The headline baseline flatters the model by about 0.11 in ΩT: a bigger domain scores the defaults worse, not better. The calibrated model barely notices — +0.007 → -0.018, +56 m → +57 m — because it stops well before the boundary. All headline numbers use 300 m, which keeps a full 105-case evaluation inside 15 seconds; this table is the correction to apply mentally when reading an absolute overshoot, and it applies almost entirely to the uncalibrated rows.

The DEM fetch failure that nearly halved the sample

Every uncached swissALTI3D tile failed with Network error: error sending request, and the first attempt at the 105-case run lost most of its cases to it. The cause was not swisstopo: this machine's system resolver cannot resolve data.geo.admin.ch at all (getaddrinfo fails after 30 s) while dig against the same nameservers answers instantly, and all four CloudFront edges respond when addressed directly. The fix used here is a 90-line local CONNECT proxy that does its own name resolution, with HTTPS_PROXY pointed at it — no repository change, TLS still validated end-to-end. It is recorded because the failure mode is indistinguishable from "swissALTI3D has no coverage there", and quietly dropping those cases would have biased the sample by region.

The p2g quantisation bias

p2g.wgsl scatters particle mass and momentum into integer atomics as u32(mass · weight · MASS_FACTOR). u32() and i32() truncate toward zero, so every scatter loses up to one quantum and the error has a consistent sign: the grid leaks mass and momentum. The fix (round instead of truncate, same cost) is committed on this branch. What it is worth, measured at the calibrated vector over all 105 avalanches:

scatter variantmass quantumΩT held outrunout error× observed area
as shipped — round()0.1 kg+0.0076-0.0017+54 m0.971×
truncate, as originally written0.1 kg+0.0074-0.0006+57 m0.970×
truncate, 100× finer quantum0.001 kg+0.0069-0.0016+56 m0.970×

All three agree to 0.0007 in ΩT and 3 m in runout distance, against a defaults-to-calibrated effect of +0.398. The arithmetic says why: at the measured slab depths and densities a particle carries a median 352 kg, and it scatters to at most nine nodes, so truncation can lose ~0.9 kg of 352 kg — a relative bias of 2.6e-03. The bug is real, it is fixed, and it was not what was wrong with these results. It would start to matter for thin slabs with many particles per cell, or at finer cell sizes, where particle mass falls.

Worth re-measuring rather than assuming: the measured storm slab is denser and deeper than a fitted 0.35 m at 200 kg/m³, which raises particle mass and therefore lowers the relative quantisation bias. Variant (iii) also raises MOMENTUM_FACTOR alongside MASS_FACTOR; that change trades overflow headroom for resolution and was reverted after the measurement rather than committed.

A latent engine bug the thickness field exposed

Simulation::set_release_areas counted release cells with thickness > 0.1 and that count sizes the particle buffer, while initialize_particles.wgsl releases from every cell > 0.01. Any release thinner than 10 cm therefore wrote more particles than were allocated. Harmless for a uniform 1 m slab, not harmless for a spatially varying field where thin cells are the normal case. Fixed to match the kernel.

Other things worth knowing

  • The entrainment flag is a no-op. use_entrainment is read in compute_particles.wgsl and never used; turning it on reproduces the default run bit-for-bit, and SimSettings::new() enables it by default.
  • The p2g truncation bias is real but immaterial (< 0.005 in ΩT), and is fixed.
  • Four terrain-path bugs — transposed non-square DEMs, missing minimum_elevation, swissALTI3D tiles the tiff crate refuses to decode, and a hardcoded 2019 acquisition year — were fixed on this branch and are what make a 105-case run possible at all.
  • Two pre-existing test failures in the data_processor ESRI ASCII parser fail identically at commit 6510004, before any change here.

11 · Against the exposé

The MSc exposé (draft 3) and the LAWIS release-area note are not redistributed here. Where this study and the planned methodology differ:

  • Outline quality is an axis the shapefile does not document. The exposé quotes Bühler et al. (2019): 33 % exact, 58 % partially estimated, 9 % expert-created. The undocumented aval_shape field reproduces that exactly (32.6 / 58.0 / 9.3 % over 18737 polygons), so it is the quality flag. This sample over-samples quality 1 on purpose — and §4 shows the score barely differs between quality bands, which is reassuring for anyone planning to use all 25 000.
  • Per-event calibration is affordable and, on these predictors, unlearnable. §7. The +0.685 skill gap is real; the clustering the MDN would need is not there.
  • The metric's distance weighting differs from its description. The exposé describes weighting cells within the observed outline by distance from the release area. The implementation weights every union cell by Euclidean distance from a single apex point. Different estimators; worth reconciling before the numbers go in a thesis.
  • ΩT = 2·IoU − 1 exactly, so the thesis can report either.
  • Slab thickness. The LAWIS note gives 30–100 cm as typical, modal bin 25–50 cm. The measured storm slab here (median 0.58 m) sits inside that, and the repo's 1.0 m default sits at its top — the shipped default is roughly a 90th-percentile slab applied to every avalanche.
  • Scale. At 94 ms a run and ~67 MB of DEM cache for 105 cases, the exposé's ~25 000 avalanches over two cycles is roughly 16 GB of terrain and, at 45 evaluations each, about 29 GPU-hours single-threaded. Tractable. The dominant cost is DEM fetching, not the solver.

12 · What I would and would not trust

Trust

  • The overshoot, and its size. Median 3.2× the observed area at defaults, decomposing into 1.53× too long and 1.65× too wide, over 105 cases spanning both size classes, all eight aspects and all three outline-quality bands. This is the robust finding of the whole study. The split between the two directions is much less robust than the total (§6).
  • That measured snowpack forcing is worth more than the parameter that stands in for it. Zero free parameters (-0.142) against the repository defaults (-0.390) and against a three-parameter fit of the same uniform-slab model (+0.005). Whatever the ranking, all three are measured the same way on the same cases (§5).
  • That per-event optima do not cluster by size, aspect or outline quality. Weak η² on a sample built to have spread in exactly those variables.
  • That the split is honest. It was fixed by hash before any simulation ran, and the calibration/held-out difference at the fitted optimum (0.014) is close to the same difference at the untuned defaults (0.089) — i.e. it is a property of which cases landed where, not of the fitting.

Do not trust

  • The tuned parameters as physics. Peak velocity at the optimum is 9 m/s median (0–49) against 27 m/s at the repository defaults; the plausible band for size 3–4 is 20–50 m/s. Extent is being matched partly by throttling the flow: the optimiser has no reason not to buy a smaller footprint with an implausibly slow avalanche, because nothing in the objective prices velocity. This is the single most important caveat in the report.
  • The absolute level of any ΩT here. The release area is inside the reference polygon and inflates α for free; read every number against the null in its row.
  • The storm slab as "the slab that released". It is ΔHS at a flat station up to 17 km away, not the wind-loaded depth in the start zone. The mass is measured; its division into depth and density is not.
  • Runout length at the default parameters, which is clipped by the domain in 102/105 cases (§10). The width numbers are far less affected.
  • The wind-loading term. One country-wide lee direction from 24-hour mean wind at stations that are not on the release slopes. It does nothing measurable and should not be believed either way.
  • Anything about deposit depth. Only planimetric extent was scored.

What I would do next, in order

  1. Switch on lateral_factor and re-score. It is a computed-then-discarded yield criterion (§6), it is a few lines, and the width metric introduced here is exactly the instrument that would show whether it helps. Treat it as a cheap experiment rather than a confident diagnosis: §6 measures the lateral bias at only 1.08.
  2. Exclude release cells from the scoring mask. That removes the degeneracy contaminating every absolute number and makes the release band safe to tune.
  3. Add a velocity term to the objective — even a weak plausibility band per size class — so the optimiser cannot buy extent with 5 m/s avalanches.
  4. Find event descriptors with signal before building the MDN (§7): start-zone curvature, channelisation index, forest cover, terrain wind exposure.
  5. Re-run at 600 m padding for anything that will be quoted as an absolute overshoot.

13 · Reproducing this

Case shapefiles and the case manifests are in data/pools/ and data/panel/; result JSON and Parquet are under campaign/. The DEM cache is fetched on demand by TileManager and is not redistributed. The commands below are run from the repository root.

cargo build --release --bin calibrate

# sample design and storm forcing (writes cases100_wx.json)
python select_sample.py
python fit_weather.py

# baseline over all 105 cases
calibrate --cases cases100_wx.json --out run100_default.json run

# the headline search: measured snowpack field, fitted on the calibration split only
calibrate --cases cases100_wx.json --split cal --out search100_wx.json \
          search --budget 70 --free mu,xi,amp --band 0.25 --slab-mode 1 --density-mode 1

# per-event calibration
calibrate --cases cases100_wx.json --out perevent100.json \
          per-event --budget 45 --free mu,xi,slab --band 0.25

# everything else
python run_matrix2.py && python render_maps100.py && python make_report.py

The swissALTI3D fetch needs the local CONNECT proxy on this machine: python dnsproxy.py & then export HTTPS_PROXY=http://127.0.0.1:8899 (§10). Case shapefiles are split out of outlines2018.shp with pyshp.
